<h2 id="2-pytorch-basics-tensors-and-autograd">2. PyTorch basics: Tensors and autograd<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#2-pytorch-basics-tensors-and-autograd"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h2>
<h3 id="what-is-a-tensor">What is a Tensor<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#what-is-a-tensor"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h3>
<p>In a Jupyter Notebook environment, install torch first, then create a sample tensor:</p>
<p><img src="/post/introtopytorchlinearregr/a571579617c9.png" alt="Creating a simple tensor"></p>
<p>This is the simplest kind of Tensor: it has no gradient and is constructed from a matrix-like array. A Tensor has several important attributes, including <code>shape</code>, <code>dtype</code>, and <code>device</code>.</p>
<ul>
<li><code>shape</code>: the shape of the matrix, i.e. how many rows and columns.</li>
<li><code>dtype</code>: the data type of the elements.</li>
<li><code>device</code>: the device the tensor lives on, typically <code>cpu</code> or <code>cuda</code>.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p>A <code>device</code> of <code>cuda</code> enables NVIDIA's CUDA, running computation on the GPU, which can significantly speed up tensor operations.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Example:</p>
<p><img src="/post/introtopytorchlinearregr/ffbe12c0bdd3.png" alt="Tensor shape, dtype and device"></p>
<p>So a Tensor is essentially a wrapper around matrix data. Let's explore its other features.</p>
<h3 id="autograd-on-tensors">Autograd on Tensors<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#autograd-on-tensors"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h3>
<p>Having looked at <strong>creating Tensors and their attributes</strong>, next comes a parameter you will use in any ML/LLM work: <code>requires_grad</code>. It is the core of creating tensors that support automatic differentiation:</p>
<p><img src="/post/introtopytorchlinearregr/521dc31c8d4e.png" alt="Tensor with requires_grad"></p>
<p><code>requires_grad</code> means that computations involving the Tensor are tracked, so that you can later differentiate with respect to it (compute gradients).</p>
<p>In the example below, we first run a series of computations on Tensors:</p>
<p><img src="/post/introtopytorchlinearregr/6f168e362648.png" alt="Computations on tensors"></p>
<p>You can see that the Tensors <code>a</code>, <code>b</code>, <code>c</code>, and <code>d</code> all have a <code>grad_fn</code> attribute, which indicates the operation that produced that Tensor.</p>
<h3 id="backpropagation-on-tensors">Backpropagation on Tensors<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#backpropagation-on-tensors"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h3>
<p>Tensor backpropagation provides the ability to trace computations, but <code>backward()</code> can only be called directly on a scalar.</p>
<p><img src="/post/introtopytorchlinearregr/55f6edf4d802.png" alt="Gradient of a scalar"></p>
<p>As shown above, the scalar <code>x</code> goes through a series of computations, and in the end its gradient (slope) can be read from the <code>.grad</code> attribute.</p>
<blockquote>
<p>Besides what is covered here, Tensors also support median, max, sum, and much more; see the <a href="https://docs.pytorch.org/docs/stable/torch.html">official documentation</a> for details.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>Defining the linear regression model takes several steps.</p>
<h3 id="step-1-prepare-the-data">Step 1: Prepare the data<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#step-1-prepare-the-data"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h3>

<blockquote>
<p>In a real scenario, <code>X</code> might be an instrument's input (the independent variable), and <code>y_true</code> the historically recorded results for each <code>X</code> (the dependent variable). This data is what the model is trained on, and in practice it would be much larger.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>PyTorch automatically builds the computation graph here and records the relationships between operations (see the Tensor section above).</p>

</code></pre></div>

<h2 id="7-backward-pass">7. Backward pass<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#7-backward-pass"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h2>
<h3 id="what-is-the-backward-pass">What is the backward pass<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#what-is-the-backward-pass"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h3>
<p>During the forward pass, a computation graph is built and the relationships between operations are recorded. The backward pass propagates in the opposite direction along that graph, computing the gradients of every parameter with <code>requires_grad=True</code>.</p>
<h3 id="the-corresponding-code-1">The corresponding code<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#the-corresponding-code-1"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h3>
<p>The backward pass is just one line:</p>
<div class="code-block-wrapper"><div class="code-block-header"><span class="code-block-lang">python</span><button class="copy-button" data-copy-state="copy" aria-label="Copy code to clipboard" type="button" onclick="copyCode(this)"><span class="copy-button-text">Copy</span></button></div><pre><code class="hljs language-python"><span class="hljs-comment"># backward pass</span>
loss.backward()
</code></pre></div>
<p>With this single call, we can compute the gradients of our two parameters, <code>W</code> and <code>b</code>.</p>
<h2 id="8-updating-the-parameters">8. Updating the parameters<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#8-updating-the-parameters"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h2>
<p>The core snippet for updating parameters:</p>
<div class="code-block-wrapper"><div class="code-block-header"><span class="code-block-lang">python</span><button class="copy-button" data-copy-state="copy" aria-label="Copy code to clipboard" type="button" onclick="copyCode(this)"><span class="copy-button-text">Copy</span></button></div><pre><code class="hljs language-python"><span class="hljs-comment"># update parameters</span>
<span class="hljs-keyword">with</span> torch.no_grad():
    W -= learning_rate * W.grad
    b -= learning_rate * b.grad

<span class="hljs-comment"># zero gradients</span>
W.grad.zero_()
b.grad.zero_()
</code></pre></div>
<p>Here we nudge the parameters to reduce the loss above, so that <code>W</code> and <code>b</code> approach their ground-truth values.</p>
<p>Two things to note:</p>
<ul>
<li>So that adjusting <code>W</code> and <code>b</code> does not get recorded in the computation graph, we use <code>torch.no_grad</code>; inside it, tensor operations are not recorded in the graph.</li>
<li>After updating the parameters, we need to reset the gradients of <code>W</code> and <code>b</code>; otherwise they accumulate into the next iteration and cause confusion.</li>
</ul>

<p>In real-world settings, training code is not usually written like this. With two parameters you can do it by hand, but LLMs have 7B, 256B, or more parameters, so writing it this way is completely impractical. torch already provides ready-made implementations of the manual computation above, and much more besides. Below is a more general approach (defining the model by subclassing <code>torch.nn.Module</code> and using torch's built-in machinery).</p>

<h2 id="10-summary">10. Summary<a aria-hidden="true" tabindex="-1" href="#10-summary"><span class="anchor-link"> #</span></a></h2>
<p>Through the hands-on example in this post, we started from <strong>the most basic mathematical model</strong>, walked through the entire process of training a linear regression model in PyTorch, and gradually opened up the "black box" of PyTorch's autograd and training mechanics.</p>
<p>The key takeaways:</p>
<ol>
<li>
<p><strong>Linear regression is not complicated at its core</strong>
Whether it is the mathematical formula</p>
<span class="katex-display"><span class="katex"><span class="katex-mathml"><math xmlns="http://www.w3.org/1998/Math/MathML" display="block"><semantics><mrow><mi>y</mi><mo>=</mo><mi>w</mi><mi>x</mi><mo>+</mo><mi>b</mi></mrow><annotation encoding="application/x-tex">y = wx + b</annotation></semantics></math></span><span class="katex-html" aria-hidden="true"><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.625em;vertical-align:-0.1944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0359em;">y</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span><span class="mrel">=</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2778em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6667em;vertical-align:-0.0833em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal" style="margin-right:0.0269em;">w</span><span class="mord mathnormal">x</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span><span class="mbin">+</span><span class="mspace" style="margin-right:0.2222em;"></span></span><span class="base"><span class="strut" style="height:0.6944em;"></span><span class="mord mathnormal">b</span></span></span></span></span>
<p>or PyTorch's <code>nn.Linear</code>, both are essentially an engineered wrapper around this one formula. Understanding that helps you keep your "model intuition" as you move on to more complex models.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>The Tensor is PyTorch's core data structure</strong>
A Tensor is more than a "multi-dimensional array": with <code>requires_grad=True</code>, it becomes a node in the computation graph and automatically participates in gradient computation, which is the foundation of PyTorch's efficient training.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Forward and backward form the training loop</strong></p>
<ul>
<li>Forward pass: compute predictions and loss from the current parameters</li>
<li>Backward pass: compute gradients by propagating the loss backward</li>
<li>Parameter update: use the gradients to update the parameters</li>
</ul>
<p>This flow applies not only to linear regression but to almost every deep learning model.</p>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>Understanding a hand-written training loop matters</strong>
Although in practice we usually use:</p>
<ul>
<li><code>nn.Module</code></li>
<li><code>torch.optim</code></li>
<li><code>nn.MSELoss</code></li>
</ul>
<p>implementing parameter updates and gradient zeroing by hand lets you truly understand:</p>
<ul>
<li>where gradients come from</li>
<li>why you need <code>zero_grad()</code></li>
<li>why parameter updates belong inside <code>torch.no_grad()</code></li>
</ul>
</li>
<li>
<p><strong>High-level abstractions are built on low-level principles</strong>
PyTorch's high-level APIs are not "magic"; they are a high-level abstraction over these basic steps. Once you understand the underlying logic, the standard style turns out to be both elegant and reliable.</p>
</li>
</ol>
<p>Overall, <strong>linear regression is the best starting point for learning PyTorch and deep learning</strong>. If you truly understand every step in this post, then multi-layer networks, CNNs, RNNs, and today's popular large language models (LLMs) all share the same training lineage.</p>
